Custom Packaging MOQ: How Order Quantity Affects Your Unit Cost

Why a 100-piece quotation can look expensive, when 200 pieces is enough, and when moving to 500 pieces actually makes sense.

Published November 23, 2026

“Can you make only 100 boxes?” is one of the questions we receive most often. Sometimes the answer is yes. The part that surprises buyers is the price: a 100-piece box is rarely half the unit price of a 200-piece order.

The reason is straightforward. Paper and board are only part of the job. We still need to check the structure and artwork, prepare materials, set up printing and die-cutting, arrange any foil or lamination, and brief the assembly team before the first finished box comes off the line.

For most custom rigid box projects, our normal starting quantity is 200 pieces per size and design. Some structures can be trialled at 100 pieces, but at a higher unit cost. Once a product is selling consistently, 500 pieces is often the point where the numbers begin to look more comfortable.

The short versionUse 100 pieces to test a product only when the structure allows it and unit price is not the first priority. Two hundred pieces is our usual starting point. Move toward 500 pieces when you have repeat sales and enough storage—not simply because the price per box is lower.

Where the MOQ Comes From

Custom packaging is made for one product; it is not pulled from a shelf of finished stock. A short production run still needs a die, print setup, colour control and an assembly plan. Several of those steps take almost the same preparation time whether the order is 100 pieces or 500.

Design & engineeringConfirm dimensions, structure, artwork and tolerances.
Printing & cutting setupPrepare plates, colours, dies and machine settings.
Finishing setupArrange foil, embossing, lamination or Spot UV processes.
Manual assemblyPrepare jigs, materials, quality points and packing workflow.

Why 100 Boxes Can Feel Expensive

Imagine that the cutting die, printing setup and sample approval cost the same before either run begins. With 100 boxes, every box carries one-hundredth of that preparation. With 500 boxes, it carries one-five-hundredth. That is the basic reason the unit price drops.

Short runs are also less efficient on the production floor. Materials may be handled manually, machines run for a brief period, and quality checks take up a larger share of the total working time. We can sometimes make 100 pieces, but we do not present it as the economical option.

Why We Normally Start at 200 Pieces

For a first proper production run, 200 pieces is usually manageable. The brand can use its own dimensions, print and finish, then see how the packaging performs with real customers without filling a warehouse.

It is still a small custom run, so it will not achieve mass-production pricing. In our experience, though, it is a more sensible place to start than paying a steep premium for 100 pieces or committing too early to a much larger batch.

When 500 Pieces Starts to Make Sense

If the product already has repeat sales, 500 pieces is often worth comparing. Setup and tooling are spread over more boxes, and material preparation is generally more efficient. The difference is especially noticeable on boxes with several finishing steps or a fitted insert.

But 500 is not automatically the “best” order. Large rigid boxes occupy real space. Artwork changes, product updates and slow sales can quickly erase the saving. We usually ask about monthly sales and available storage before recommending the larger quantity.

One Detail Buyers Often Miss: MOQ Is Per Size and Design

Five hundred boxes split across five sizes is not the same production job as 500 identical boxes. Each size can need its own die and setup. Different artwork, colours or insert layouts may also need separate preparation, so the quantity cannot always be combined.

Ways to Keep the Price Sensible

  • Use one well-planned size instead of several very similar sizes.
  • Choose one strong finish rather than combining foil, embossing, Spot UV and multiple laminations.
  • Right-size the box to reduce board use and volumetric freight.
  • Consider a foldable structure for large rigid boxes shipped internationally.
  • Use a paperboard insert where heavy foam protection is unnecessary.
  • Prepare accurate artwork and specifications before sampling to avoid revisions.

A Lower Unit Price Is Not Always a Lower Total Cost

A cheaper box is not a saving if half the order remains unused. Sampling, tooling, freight, import costs, storage and the chance of a product or artwork change all belong in the calculation.

For a new launch, we may still recommend 200 pieces even when the 500-piece quotation looks better on paper. For a proven product that sells every month, the opposite may be true.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the standard MOQ for custom packaging?

Our standard production MOQ is 200 pieces per custom size and design for most custom rigid box projects. Paper bags and some special structures may require a higher minimum.

Can I order only 100 custom boxes?

Selected structures may support a 100-piece trial run, but availability must be confirmed and the unit cost is higher because setup and tooling costs are divided across fewer pieces.

Why is 500 pieces more cost-effective?

Printing, die-cutting, sampling and production setup costs are spread across more units. Material preparation and production scheduling may also become more efficient.

Can I divide the MOQ among different sizes?

Usually each custom size requires separate tooling and setup, so the MOQ generally applies per size. We can review whether one outer structure with variable inserts is practical.

Does every colour count as a separate MOQ?

Different artwork or colours can require separate printing and material preparation. The treatment depends on the printing method and specification.

Does the sample count toward the MOQ?

A custom sample is normally produced and charged separately before mass production. It is used to confirm structure, size, colour, finish and insert fit.

How can I get an accurate price comparison?

Provide one complete specification and ask for the same box at two quantities, such as 200 and 500 pieces. This creates a meaningful comparison because the size, material and finishing remain consistent.
